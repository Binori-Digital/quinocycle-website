Every order your team ships starts with someone walking to a shelf and picking stock — and in most small warehouses, that walking eats up more time than the packing itself. Industry estimates put travel at around half of total picking time. Choosing the right picking method is the cheapest productivity upgrade a Malaysian warehouse can make: no software, no racking overhaul, just a better system.

## The 4 Main Warehouse Picking Methods Compared

| Method | How it works | Best for | Order volume | Error risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single order | Pick one order start to finish | Startups, bulky or custom items | Under ~20/day | Lowest |
| Batch | Pick 5–15 orders in one trip, sort at packing table | Shopee/Lazada sellers, similar SKUs | 20–100/day | Medium — needs sorting discipline |
| Zone | Each picker owns an area; orders pass between zones | Larger teams, wide SKU range | 100+/day | Low per zone, handoffs add risk |
| Wave | Batches released on a schedule (by courier cutoff, route) | Multiple couriers, strict SLAs | 50+/day | Medium — timing errors |

## Single Order Picking: Simple, Honest, Slow

One picker, one order, one trip. It is the default everyone starts with, and for good reason: nothing gets mixed up, and training takes five minutes. If you ship fewer than 20 orders a day, or your products are bulky one-item orders (furniture, cargo cartons), stay here — the fancier methods add complexity you do not need yet.

The weakness shows as volume grows: 50 orders means 50 round trips past the same shelves. That is when walking time explodes.

## Batch Picking: The SME Sweet Spot

Batch picking groups similar orders — say, 10 orders that all contain items from the same two aisles — into a single trip. The picker carries a trolley or a large carton divided into slots, picks everything in one pass, then sorts items into individual orders at the packing table.

For a typical Shopee or TikTok Shop seller doing 30–80 orders daily, batch picking commonly cuts walking time by around half. The discipline that makes it work: never sort in the aisle, and cap batches at what one trolley carries reliably — over-stuffed batches are where mispicks breed.

## Zone and Wave Picking: When You Grow Into Them

**Zone picking** assigns each picker a fixed area — one owns aisles A–B, another C–D — and orders travel between zones. It shines past 100 orders a day with a wide SKU range, because each picker memorises their zone and stops crossing the warehouse. The catch is handoffs: each zone-to-zone pass is a chance to lose a bin, so it needs clear passing rules.

**Wave picking** layers a schedule on top of batching: release a wave at 11am for the 1pm Pos Laju pickup, another at 3pm for the 5pm J&T cutoff. If you juggle multiple couriers with different collection times — the daily reality of Malaysian marketplace selling — waves stop the 4.45pm panic. Start simple: two waves a day, timed to your two biggest courier pickups.

## FIFO vs LIFO for Cartoned Stock

How you stack cartons decides which picking order is even possible.

**FIFO (first in, first out)** — oldest stock ships first. Non-negotiable for food, cosmetics, anything with expiry or warranty periods, and honestly wise for everything else: cartons stored long-term collect dust and yellow under fluorescent light, and customers judge the box. FIFO needs a date-in marked on every carton and racking you can load from the back or rotate front-to-back.

**LIFO (last in, first out)** — newest on top ships first. Acceptable only for identical, non-perishable stock where rotation genuinely is not worth the labour of restacking floor-stacked columns.

If your cartons sit more than a couple of months, FIFO with a marker-pen date on each box costs nothing and prevents shipping shabby stock. Weight also matters here — heavy cartons low, light on top, which conveniently suits FIFO rotation on racking. ## Pick-Path Basics That Cost Nothing

- **Number locations in walking order** (A1, A2, A3… down one aisle, back up the next) and sort every pick list by location code. The route optimises itself.
- **Put your fastest movers nearest the packing table.** In most SME warehouses, roughly 20% of SKUs generate 80% of picks — those belong within a few steps of packing.
- **One SKU per location.** Mixed locations are the top cause of mispicks in un-softwared warehouses.
- **Face labels outward.** A carton whose label faces the wall forces the picker to drag it out just to read it.
- **Keep the aisle clear of "temporary" stock.** Cartons parked in walkways break the fixed route and force detours — every detour is walking time and a tripping hazard.
- **Batch your replenishment.** Top up pick locations once or twice a day from bulk storage instead of interrupting pickers mid-route whenever a shelf runs empty.
