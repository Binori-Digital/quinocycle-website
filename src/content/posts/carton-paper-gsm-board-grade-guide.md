Two cartons can look identical on the shelf, yet one collapses under 15kg while the other shrugs off 30kg. The difference is invisible: the grade and weight of the paper it's made from. Understanding GSM and board grades takes ten minutes to learn and permanently changes how you buy boxes — because you'll finally know what you're paying for (and what a supplier might be quietly leaving out).

## GSM: The Number Behind Paper Weight

**GSM = grams per square metre** — how much one square metre of that paper layer weighs. It's the same measure used for office paper (typically 70–80 GSM) and premium name cards (300+ GSM).

In corrugated cartons, GSM is quoted per layer:

- **Liners** (the flat outer and inner faces): commonly **125, 150, 200 or 230 GSM** in Malaysia.
- **Medium/fluting** (the wavy layer between liners): commonly **105–150 GSM**.

Heavier paper means more fibre per square metre — stiffer, more puncture-resistant, better stacking strength, and of course more expensive. Moving from 125 GSM to 150 GSM liners typically adds a meaningful chunk to board cost, which is why budget cartons cluster at the lower weights.

## Kraft vs Test Liner: The Letter Before the Number

GSM alone doesn't tell you strength, because not all paper fibre is equal:

- **Kraft liner (K)** — made predominantly from virgin softwood pulp. Long fibres interlock strongly, so kraft delivers the best strength-to-weight and better humidity resistance. Usually the smooth, even brown surface on quality boxes.
- **Test liner (T)** — made largely from recycled fibre. Shorter fibres, so it's weaker at the same GSM, but noticeably cheaper and more eco-friendly. Perfectly fine for lighter duties.
- **Medium (M)** — the fluting paper, almost always recycled-based, graded by GSM.

A common cost-smart construction is kraft on the outside (where strength, printing and weather exposure matter) with test liner inside. At the same 150 GSM, a kraft liner can be substantially stronger than a test liner — which is exactly why two "150 GSM boxes" can perform very differently.

## How to Read a Board Grade Like "K150/M125/K150"

Board grades read layer by layer, outermost first:

| Spec | Meaning | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| T125/M105/T125 | Recycled liners 125 GSM, light flute | Budget e-commerce parcels, light goods |
| K125/M105/T125 | Kraft face, recycled inner | Everyday single wall cartons |
| K150/M125/K150 | Kraft both faces, 150 GSM | Quality single wall — parcels up to ~15kg |
| K150/M125/T125/M125/K150 | Five layers = double wall | Moving boxes, fragile goods, stacking |
| K200/M150/T150/M150/K200 | Heavy double wall | Heavy duty, cargo, export cartons |

Notice the five-part specs: those are **double wall** boards — two flute layers, three liners. Wall count is its own subject with its own trade-offs; our [single wall vs double wall guide](/blog/single-wall-vs-double-wall-vs-triple-wall-carton/) covers when the second wall is worth paying for. And the *shape* of the flute (A, B, C or E profile) adds another dimension entirely — that's covered in our [flute types guide](/blog/carton-box-flute-types-guide-0413/).

> **Quick field test:** press your thumb firmly into the face of a carton. Quality kraft liner at decent GSM springs back with barely a mark; low-GSM recycled liner dents and stays dented. It's not a lab test, but it catches the worst offenders in two seconds.

## GSM Is a Recipe — Performance Tests Are the Proof

Here's the part many buyers miss: GSM and grade describe the *ingredients*, not the *result*. Actual box performance depends on fibre quality, glue lines, manufacturing precision and humidity. That's why the industry also uses performance tests — **ECT** (edge crush test, predicts stacking strength) and the **Mullen burst test** (puncture/burst resistance). If you're comparing suppliers seriously, ask for those numbers too; our [ECT vs Mullen explainer](/blog/packaging-knowledge-ect-mullen-burst-test-0429/) breaks down what each score means and which one matters for your use case.

The practical takeaway: **specs are for comparing quotes; tests are for verifying claims.** A supplier who can tell you both is a supplier who knows their product.

One more factor that matters especially in Malaysia: humidity. Paper loses stiffness as it absorbs moisture from the air, and our year-round humidity sits high enough to noticeably soften low-grade board. Kraft liner's longer virgin fibres hold up better in damp conditions than recycled test liner at the same GSM — one more reason the letter in the spec matters as much as the number, particularly for goods stored through monsoon season or shipped to the east coast.

GSM and grade are the main reason carton prices vary so much for the "same size" box. A few honest rules of thumb from the warehouse floor:
