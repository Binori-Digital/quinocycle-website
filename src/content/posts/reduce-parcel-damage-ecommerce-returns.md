Every damaged parcel costs you three times: the refund or replacement, the return shipping, and the one-star review that outlives both. Yet most parcel damage is boringly preventable — not with expensive materials, but with a handful of techniques that add sen, not ringgit, to each order. Here are the seven fixes that matter, roughly in order of impact.

## Why Parcels Actually Get Damaged

Between your packing table and the buyer's doorstep, a parcel is loaded, sorted, conveyed, stacked and unloaded many times. Realistically, it will be dropped at least once and stacked under heavier boxes more than once. Damage almost always traces back to one of three failures:

- **Contents shifting** inside a box with empty space — the product hits the wall so the wall doesn't have to hit the product.
- **Box crushing** under stacked weight — a single-wall box at the bottom of a courier cage carries everything above it.
- **Corner and edge impacts** — boxes land on corners, and corners are where most boxes are weakest.

Each fix below targets one of these. None require custom packaging.

## Fix 1: The 5 cm Void Rule

The most valuable habit in parcel packing: aim for about **5 cm of cushioned space between the product and every wall of the box**, then fill every remaining gap completely. The test is simple — close the box and shake it. If you hear or feel movement, it's not done.

Scrunched kraft paper is the cheapest void fill; bubble wrap earns its cost on genuine fragiles. What you're building is suspension: the product should float in the middle of the box, never touching a wall it could be slammed against.

## Fix 2: Double Wall for Fragile SKUs

If one line in this article saves you money, it's this one. For fragile products — glass, ceramics, electronics, bottled liquids — move to **double-wall cartons**. Two corrugated layers roughly double crush and stacking strength, which is precisely what protects the bottom box in a stacked courier cage.

## Fix 3: H-Tape Every Parcel

Look at the top of a carton: one centre seam, two edge seams. **H-taping** covers all three — one strip along the centre, one across each end — forming an H. Do the same on the bottom.

It uses roughly 50% more tape (still only sen per parcel) and delivers three things: flaps that cannot pop open under stacking pressure, sealed edges that keep out dust and rain-splash during monsoon-season deliveries, and noticeably stiffer box ends. A single centre strip is the minimum; H-taping is the standard for anything heavy, fragile or valuable.

## Fix 4: Build Crush Zones at the Corners

Corners take the hardest hits and buckle first. Two cheap upgrades:

- **Corner padding:** fold cardboard offcuts into L-shaped angles (or double-fold kraft paper) and seat them inside each corner before packing. You're giving the impact something sacrificial to crush.
- **Never let the product occupy a corner.** The 5 cm rule already prevents this, but it's worth stating: the corner of your product should never sit in the corner of your box.

Flattened old cartons make free corner-protection stock — one more reason a box's life doesn't end at first use.

## Fix 5: Drop-Test Your Own Parcel

Before you ship SKU number 500, sacrifice one parcel. Pack it exactly as you normally do, then **drop it from waist height** — about 80–100 cm — once flat, once on an edge, once on a corner. Open it and inspect.

This crude test approximates normal courier handling surprisingly well. If your product survives, ship with confidence. If it doesn't, you just paid one parcel to learn what fifty angry customers would have taught you slower and more expensively.

> **Test again whenever anything changes:** new box supplier, new filler, new product variant, even a new packer on the team. Damage rates usually spike right after a "small" packaging change nobody thought to re-test.

## Fix 6: Understand What Damage Does to Your Ratings

On Shopee, TikTok Shop and Lazada, damaged-parcel complaints hit you as one-star reviews, refund disputes and return shipments — and ratings feed directly into search ranking, badge eligibility and ad performance. The damage itself costs money once; the rating damage suppresses sales for months.

That's the context for treating packaging as a growth lever, not a cost line. A seller whose damage complaints drop visibly usually sees review scores — and conversion — recover within a review cycle or two.

## Fix 7: Do the RM1 vs RM15 Maths

Here's the worked estimate that settles most "is the better box worth it?" debates. Suppose upgrading a fragile SKU from a single-wall to a double-wall box costs **RM1 more per parcel**, and a damage-driven return costs you roughly:

| Cost of one damaged parcel | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Refund or replacement product | RM8–RM40+ |
| Return/reship courier fees | RM6–RM10 |
| Time handling the dispute | 20–30 min |
| Rating and visibility impact | Hard to price, real |
| **Typical total** | **RM15–RM50** |

At even a conservative **RM15 per damage**, the RM1 upgrade pays for itself if it prevents just **one damage per 15 parcels shipped** — and in practice, moving fragile SKUs to proper double-wall boxes with full void fill cuts damage by far more than that. The maths only says no when your product was never at risk (clothing, soft goods) — in which case, spend the ringgit nowhere and keep it.
